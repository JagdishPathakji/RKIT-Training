RSA AND PUBLIC-KEY CRYPTOGRAPHY
===============================

This file begins with the problem of sharing a secret, introduces public and
private keys, and then explains RSA-2048, encryption, and digital signatures.
The examples refer to knowledge-base article exports.


1. THE KEY-SHARING PROBLEM
--------------------------

AES uses one secret key for both encryption and decryption. That works well
when one application encrypts and decrypts its own data, but sharing that
secret with another party creates a problem: how can the parties exchange the
secret without an eavesdropper obtaining it?

Public-key cryptography uses a related pair of keys:

    Public key  - may be shared with other people or services.
    Private key - kept secret by its owner.

The keys are mathematically related, but knowing the public key should not
make it practical to calculate the private key.

RSA is one public-key algorithm. It can be used for encryption and for digital
signatures, with different operations and padding schemes.


2. IMPORTANT TERMS
------------------

Key pair
    The public key and matching private key created together.

Public key
    The part distributed to people who need to encrypt to the owner or verify
    the owner's signatures. It is not secret, but recipients still need a
    trustworthy way to know it belongs to the claimed owner.

Private key
    The secret part that decrypts data sent to its owner or creates signatures.
    If stolen, an attacker may decrypt protected messages or impersonate the
    signer. Protect it using a secure key store or hardware-backed service
    where appropriate.

Modulus
    A large number inside RSA's key mathematics. The RSA key size is the bit
    length of the modulus.

RSA-2048
    RSA with a 2048-bit modulus. This does not mean a 2048-character key or a
    2048-bit password. RSA-2048 has a 256-byte modulus size. Its security
    strength is not equivalent to a 2048-bit AES key; comparing bit lengths
    across algorithms is misleading.

Padding
    A defined method that adds structure/randomness around RSA's input before
    the RSA calculation. Raw RSA must not be used directly. Correct padding is
    essential for security.

Hash
    A fixed-length digest calculated from data. SHA-256 is commonly used with
    RSA signatures. A hash is not encryption and cannot be decrypted to get
    the original content.

Digital signature
    A value created with a private key over data (usually via a hash). Anyone
    with the matching public key can check whether the data matches that
    signature.

Certificate
    A signed document that connects a public key to an identity or name. RSA
    keys alone do not prove that a key belongs to a claimed organization.


3. RSA ENCRYPTION AND RSA SIGNATURES ARE DIFFERENT
-------------------------------------------------

Encryption for a recipient:

    Sender encrypts using recipient's public key.
    Recipient decrypts using their private key.

Signature by an author:

    Author signs using their private key.
    Verifier checks using author's public key.

Do not describe a signature as “encrypting with the private key.” Signing and
encryption are different operations with different security properties and
padding. A signature does not hide the article. RSA encryption does not prove
who created the encrypted message.

What a valid signature means:
    The checked data matches a signature made by the holder of the corresponding
    private key (assuming the public key is the expected one).

What it does not automatically mean:
    - A particular human personally approved the article.
    - The key was not stolen.
    - The article is true or safe.
    - The public key genuinely belongs to the named signer unless that
      association is established and trusted.


4. HOW RSA WORKS AT A HIGH LEVEL
--------------------------------

RSA begins by generating two large secret prime numbers. The implementation
uses them to calculate a public key and a matching private key. The primes
must remain secret and are not normally exposed to application code.

The public and private values support a mathematical relationship:

    - A public-key operation can be reversed by the matching private key.
    - A private-key signature operation can be checked by the matching public
      key.

The exact equations involve modular arithmetic and carefully chosen
parameters. .NET's cryptographic provider performs these calculations. An
application developer should use a reviewed library API rather than writing
RSA mathematics or padding by hand.

RSA security depends on the difficulty of factoring the large modulus back
into its secret prime factors. Increasing key size makes attacks more
difficult but also increases operation cost and signature/ciphertext size.


5. WHY 2048 BITS, AND WHAT IT DOES NOT MEAN
------------------------------------------

RSA-2048 is a widely supported baseline for many current applications, subject
to an organization's security policy and the expected lifetime of the data.
Some policies require larger keys or different algorithms. Follow current
organizational and platform guidance for the system's required lifetime.

The modulus is 2048 bits = 256 bytes. Therefore:
    - An RSA-2048 signature is 256 bytes.
    - RSA-2048 ciphertext is also 256 bytes.
    - These lengths are fixed by the modulus size, regardless of plaintext
      length for ciphertext.

RSA does not encrypt arbitrary amounts of content. With OAEP and SHA-256, the
maximum message size is:

    modulusBytes - 2 * hashBytes - 2
    256 - 2 * 32 - 2 = 190 bytes for RSA-2048

That limit is far too small for a normal JSON article. Use RSA to protect a
small AES key, or use RSA to sign article bytes. Do not try to encrypt a whole
article with RSA.


6. RSA ENCRYPTION PADDING: OAEP
-------------------------------

OAEP means Optimal Asymmetric Encryption Padding. It is the recommended
padding family for new RSA encryption designs in .NET.

Use RSA-OAEP with SHA-256 (in .NET: RSAEncryptionPadding.OaepSHA256) where
compatible. Do not use raw RSA. Avoid PKCS#1 v1.5 encryption for new designs
unless a legacy protocol requires it and has been reviewed.

RSA encryption is randomized: encrypting the same plaintext twice with the
same public key and OAEP should normally produce different ciphertexts.
Ciphertext should be decrypted only by the private key holder.

OAEP solves padding/security problems; it does not make RSA suitable for large
data. The size limit still applies.


7. RSA SIGNATURE PADDING: PSS
-----------------------------

PSS means Probabilistic Signature Scheme. It is a modern RSA signature padding
method and a preferred choice for new signatures when both sides support it.

In .NET, use:

    HashAlgorithmName.SHA256
    RSASignaturePadding.Pss

Signing the article computes a hash and applies a private-key operation to
produce a signature. Verification checks the signature against the article
bytes and public key. If even one article byte changes, verification should
fail.

PKCS#1 v1.5 signatures can be needed for compatibility with older systems.
This is different from using PKCS#1 v1.5 for encryption. Choose a standard
scheme for the exact operation; do not interchange encryption and signature
padding.


8. WHEN RSA FITS THE KNOWLEDGE-BASE PROJECT
-------------------------------------------

Signing:
    Your service can sign a published article export with its private key.
    External consumers can verify it with a trusted public key. This helps
    detect changes and establish that the signer controlled the key.

Confidential delivery:
    A sender can encrypt a small AES key for a recipient's public key. The
    recipient decrypts that AES key with the private key and uses AES-GCM for
    the article. This is hybrid encryption, explained in file 08.

Usually not appropriate:
    - Encrypting the entire article directly with RSA.
    - Using RSA to store user passwords.
    - Assuming any public key presented by a sender belongs to your service.
    - Using a signature as a replacement for article review approval records.


9. PUBLIC KEY TRUST AND IDENTITY
-------------------------------

A public key is shareable, but a receiver must know it is the right public
key. If an attacker substitutes their own key and the application accepts it,
the cryptographic operation can succeed with the attacker's key.

Possible trust mechanisms include:
    - A public key distributed through a trusted, authenticated channel.
    - A certificate checked under an accepted certificate authority.
    - A pinned public key or fingerprint distributed out of band.
    - A managed enterprise key directory.

A fingerprint is a short display/check value derived from a public key. It
helps people compare keys but requires a trusted way to exchange the expected
fingerprint.


10. COMMON MISTAKES
-------------------

- Treating a public key as proof of identity without validating it.
- Sharing or logging a private key.
- Using raw RSA or inventing custom padding.
- Encrypting a long article directly with RSA.
- Using encryption to claim who authored content.
- Calling a digital signature encryption.
- Using the wrong padding for the operation.
- Assuming RSA-2048 means “2048-bit security.”
- Assuming a valid signature proves human intent or article correctness.
- Forgetting how the private key will be backed up, rotated, and recovered.


11. MENTAL MODEL
----------------

RSA creates a pair: share the public key, protect the private key.
Encrypt to a recipient with their public key; only their private key decrypts.
Sign with the author's private key; verify with the author's public key.
RSA is for small data or signatures, not whole article files.
Trusting the public key is a separate identity problem.
